Scientific illustration has split into two camps. **Icon-library tools** (BioRender, Mind the Graph) give you thousands of pre-drawn cells, proteins and lab equipment to drag onto a canvas. **AI figure makers** (SciDrawer, Illustrae, FigureLabs) draw the figure for you from a text prompt, a sketch or a reference image. Behind both sit the classic **vector editors** (Illustrator, Inkscape) and the free **asset libraries** many labs still rely on.

This guide compares the ten tools researchers ask about most, by what they are actually good at: mechanism and pathway diagrams, graphical abstracts, biological illustration, chemistry, or final polishing for a journal.

## 1. SciDrawer: best for AI mechanism and pathway figures

[SciDrawer](/generate) is an AI scientific figure maker built for the figures researchers redraw most: signaling pathways, mechanisms of action, experimental workflows and [graphical abstracts](/graphical-abstract-maker). You can start three ways:

- **Text prompt**: describe the mechanism ("TNF-α binds TNFR1, recruits TRADD…") and get a labeled diagram.
- **Sketch**: photograph a whiteboard or paper drawing; SciDrawer keeps your layout and redraws it with clean connectors and aligned labels.
- **Reference figure**: upload a figure whose style you like and generate new panels with a matching palette and visual language, so every figure in a manuscript looks consistent.

It renders only what you describe and leaves gaps blank rather than inventing biology, which matters when a reviewer checks every arrow. Ready-made starting points live in the [template gallery](/templates).

**Pros:** fastest route from an idea to a finished research figure; sketch and style-matching inputs; commercial and journal use included; English and Chinese interface.
**Cons:** output is a high-resolution raster image, not a layered vector file. For node-level edits you regenerate or finish the figure in Illustrator, Inkscape or Figma.
**Pricing:** free starter credits on sign-up; paid plans from $9/month, plus one-off credit packs from $5. See [pricing](/pricing).

## 2. BioRender: best icon library for biology

BioRender is the reference point for biological illustration software. Its strength is a very large, scientifically reviewed icon library and templates that many journals' readers recognize at a glance. Everything is drag-and-drop, so you get precise control, but complex pathways take time to assemble by hand.

**Pros:** huge, consistent icon set; templates; team collaboration.
**Cons:** manual assembly is slow for large figures; the free plan does **not** allow publication or commercial use.
**Pricing:** free plan without publication rights; the academic Individual plan is listed at $35/month billed annually ($39 monthly), and industry plans cost considerably more.

## 3. Illustrae: best budget BioRender-style canvas with AI

Illustrae combines an infinite canvas with an icon library and AI-generated custom elements: describe something that isn't in the library and it generates a matching asset. It is popular with students and small labs who want a BioRender-like experience for less money, and it exports SVG, EPS and PNG.

**Pros:** low entry price; AI elements fill gaps in the icon library; vector export.
**Cons:** you still compose the figure yourself, so it is less automatic than full text-to-figure tools.
**Pricing:** paid plans start under $10/month, with unlimited and one-time lifetime tiers. Check the current limits on generations and canvases.

## 4. FigureLabs: AI figures with vector export

FigureLabs generates illustrations, flowcharts and plots from text, sketches, photos or data files, and can convert results into editable SVG. Its canvas supports redrawing regions, recoloring and removing backgrounds.

**Pros:** many input types; vectorization; editable PPTX export.
**Cons:** credit-based plans need some planning; the free tier is non-commercial and capped at 1K resolution.
**Pricing:** free plan (non-commercial); Starter $9/month; Plus and Pro tiers above that. If you are comparing it with other AI tools, see our [FigureLabs alternatives](/blog/figurelabs-alternatives) guide.

## 5. Mind the Graph: best for infographic-style visual abstracts

Mind the Graph focuses on templates for infographics, posters and visual abstracts, with a scientific illustration library behind them. It suits researchers who want a polished, "designed" look without starting from scratch.

**Pros:** strong templates for posters and abstracts; easy for non-designers.
**Cons:** less suited to dense mechanism diagrams; the free plan is limited, so check the watermark and resolution terms before publishing.

## 6. Adobe Illustrator: best for final polishing and journal covers

Illustrator remains the industry standard for print-quality vector work, so most journal art departments expect its files. Use it to finish figures: align panels, fix fonts to journal specs and export the exact format required.

**Pros:** total control; perfect print output.
**Cons:** steep learning curve; subscription cost; slow for building figures from nothing.

## 7. Inkscape: best free vector editor

Inkscape is open source and handles SVG natively. Pair it with free icon libraries (below) or with AI-generated drafts to get a zero-cost pipeline for publication figures.

**Pros:** free forever; capable vector tools; cross-platform.
**Cons:** the interface takes time to learn; no built-in scientific icons.

## 8. Servier Medical Art and Bioicons: best free biological illustration assets

If you need free biological illustration software, start with the assets rather than the editor. **Servier Medical Art** offers thousands of medical and biological drawings under a Creative Commons license, and **Bioicons** collects open-licensed SVG icons for biology and chemistry. Drop them into Inkscape, PowerPoint or Figma.

**Pros:** free; easy to edit; widely used in papers.
**Cons:** you must follow each license's attribution terms; styles vary between sets.

## 9. ChemDraw: best for chemical structures

For chemistry, nothing replaces a tool that understands bonds and valences. ChemDraw draws structures and reaction mechanisms to IUPAC conventions and exports to the formats chemistry journals expect. Many universities provide institutional licenses.

**Pros:** chemically aware; industry-standard `.cdx` files.
**Cons:** chemistry only; expensive without an institutional license.

## 10. PowerPoint and Figma: best for assembling panels

Plenty of published figures are still assembled in PowerPoint or Figma. They don't draw biology for you, but they are fast for laying out multi-panel figures, adding labels and preparing slides, and most researchers already have access.

## Which scientific illustration tool should you choose?

- **You want a finished mechanism, pathway or graphical abstract fast:** an AI figure maker. [Try SciDrawer](/generate) with a text prompt or a photo of your sketch.
- **You need standardized biology icons and full manual control:** BioRender, or Illustrae if budget matters.
- **You need free biological illustration software:** Inkscape plus Servier Medical Art or Bioicons.
- **You draw chemistry:** ChemDraw.
- **You are finishing a figure for a journal or a cover:** Illustrator or Inkscape.

Most labs combine two: an AI tool or icon library to build the figure, and a vector editor to finish it to journal specifications.

## FAQ

**What is the best AI tool for scientific illustration?**
For mechanism, pathway and graphical-abstract figures generated from a description or sketch, AI figure makers such as SciDrawer, FigureLabs and Illustrae are the fastest options. SciDrawer focuses on finished, journal-ready figures from text, sketches and reference images.

**Is there free biological illustration software?**
Yes. Inkscape is a free, open-source vector editor, and Servier Medical Art and Bioicons provide free biological icons under Creative Commons licenses. SciDrawer and FigureLabs also give new users free credits.

**Can I publish figures made with free plans?**
It depends on the tool. BioRender's free plan does not allow publication, and FigureLabs' free plan is non-commercial. SciDrawer figures are yours to publish, including commercially. Always read the license before you submit.

**What is the best Illustrae alternative?**
If you want the figure generated for you rather than composed from icons, try SciDrawer or FigureLabs. If you want the icon-library workflow, BioRender is the main alternative.
